How it works

From the courier bag to the archive fridge.

One station on the bench you already have, wired into your LIS, middleware and instruments. Here it is running every step.

Before the analyzerAnalyzerAfter
  1. Opens the sealed courier bag and pulls out the tubes and the requisition.
  2. Reads label, tube type, fill and integrity, and flags any conflict with the order.
  3. Checks each accepted tube into the LIS, linked to its order.
  4. Routes to spin, no-spin or hold. Held tubes wait for a person.
  5. Balances the rack, runs the right protocol, unloads when it stops.
  6. Decaps, splits primaries into labelled daughter tubes and loads analyzer racks.
  7. Stays exactly as it is. Loaded in the order your middleware expects.
  8. Pulls racks the moment results release and closes every tube.
  9. Stores each tube in indexed fridge racks, its position written to the LIS.
  10. Finds any tube for an add-on or rerun, and clears expired ones by policy.

  1. Before the analyzer
  8. At the analyzer
  10. After the analyzer

Held specimens are deliberately not accessioned, so nothing downstream can pull, bill or treat them as received until a person rules on them.

Every job is checked before and verified after: order, tube and destination must agree before a tube moves, and cameras confirm the result before the LIS is updated.

Receiving runs on the bench prototype today. Each further step is a new trained skill on the same two arms.

The problem, in numbers

The work around the analyzer is where the errors start and the hours go.

of laboratory errors happen before testing ever starts.

Most lab error is pre-analytical: the wrong tube for the order, a specimen that was never received into the LIS, a tube spun that should not have been. The analyzer is rarely the problem. The bench that creates the problem is the one still run entirely by hand.
